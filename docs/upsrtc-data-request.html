Why we are asking

We are building a control system that helps depot and control-room staff keep buses running to time, evenly spaced through the day, and evenly loaded rather than one bus crush-full behind an empty one. It already reads the live GPS feed and detects when two buses have closed up on each other.

What it cannot currently do is tell whether a bus is late, measure spacing at the stop where passengers actually wait, or see how many people are on board. Those three gaps are all data gaps, not software gaps. The requests below close them.

Every model underneath this — how long a bus dwells at a stop, how long a link takes at 09:00 versus 16:00, how many passengers accumulate between buses — has to be fitted from history. We hold none, because the live feed is a current-position snapshot that we overwrite on every poll. That is why item 1 leads the list.

2

Ticketing / ETM transaction log

Per-ticket records with boarding and alighting stops

The only route to load balancing

This is how we learn how many passengers are on each bus. The boarding stop gives us boardings per stop; the destination stop gives us alightings; the running difference reconstructs the load profile along the whole trip. It also tells us how fast passengers accumulate at each stop, which is the one number that decides whether holding a bus is worth it at all.

6

Which stops a bus may safely wait at

Depot and traffic knowledge, not a system export

Roughly 20–30 stops per corridor

The system's main tool is asking a driver to wait a little longer at a stop so the gap behind can close. That is only acceptable at stops where a waiting bus does not block traffic. Getting this wrong is the fastest way to lose driver cooperation, and no data feed contains it — it comes from depot managers and traffic staff.

For each stop on the pilot corridors, we need a plain answer to: can a bus wait here for up to ten minutes without causing a problem? Useful flags alongside it:

  • Has a lay-by or bus bay, versus stopping in the running lane
  • Sits immediately before a signalised junction — drivers will not wait through a green
  • Narrow carriageway or a known congestion point
  • Terminal or major interchange, where waiting is normal and expected
7

Duty and block roster

Please confirm rather than extract, in the first instance

May already be covered

To say a trip is late we need to know which bus was scheduled to run it. The live feed already carries vj_id against each vehicle, so this may already be available to us. Before anyone builds an export, we would like to confirm one thing: is vj_id in the live GPS feed the scheduled journey, or is it assigned when the bus actually starts running?

If it is assigned at run time, we would then need the planned roster: vj_id → scheduled reg_num, per service day, for the archive window.

How we would like it delivered

  • Keep the source identifiers. Registration numbers, journey ids and stop codes exactly as your systems hold them. We join these against the live feed, so any re-keying or tidying breaks the join.
  • Do not pre-aggregate. Please send per-event rows, not hourly or daily summaries. The whole method depends on individual events.
  • Keep incomplete rows. Send records with missing fields rather than dropping them. Where coverage is patchy, that is something we need to know rather than have hidden.
  • Delivery by SFTP drop, a cloud storage bucket, or a one-time archive link — whichever suits your team. Gzipped CSV, NDJSON or Parquet all work.
  • A data dictionary for each export, however rough, saves considerable back-and-forth over field meanings and units.

If the full request is too large to start with

Send items 1 and 2 for a single corridor over two weeks. That alone lets us establish how much of the bunching on that corridor is caused by passenger demand versus by irregular departures from the terminal — which decides whether en-route holding is the right tool at all, or whether regulating departures from the origin gets most of the benefit at a fraction of the operational disruption.
